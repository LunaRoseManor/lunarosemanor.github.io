---
title: Soul Cemetary
category: review
tags: ttrpg, rpg, positive
---

It's hard to admit, but I can't help but feel burned out on video games. As of late, it seems like I choose to spend my time elsewhere. The problem isn't with the hobby or even the surrounding industry, it's me. I've changed. Part of this is to do with time. I find the amount of investment a lot of games are asking of my adult self is a little unreasonable in terms of keeping up with everything. I'd much rather experience them second hand through reviews or wait until all the hype has died down to play them at my own time and pace. Sometimes to my personal detriment. This didn't used to be true however. There was a time a decade or so ago where gaming on electronics was both my life and personality. Where I put at least a little stock and pride in knowing all the stats of all the different Pokémon or checking PC Gamer for news on the latest releases. My playthrough of Bloodborne has taken over a year and feels somewhat like an unfinished project. More work than activity.

In a way I suppose nothing has changed. My branching out into physical gaming with TTRPGs and board games feels like a natural evolution in many ways and has put me in touch with art I'd never have considered otherwise.

This includes Soul Cemetary.

I'm going to make something absolutely fucking clear. I don't know where to start with this one because I'm not sure what it even is. Yet despite this I'm gagging to talk about it and sing its praises. Not knowing where to begin or how to engage with it might even be the point, in fact. It's a deeply metatextual work that challenges both the trend of fake video game art at time of writing recently exploding online, people's nostalgia for stuff they experienced in times past and what artistic mediums even are. Though I'm perhaps getting ahead of myself. The store page says it's a solo TTRPG and I'm going to try and review it as that. But unlike say, Apothecaria there's a continued resistance to teach you how exactly you play it. It starts off giving you clear instructions but lots of detail is left vague.

How, exactly, am I supposed to "react" as a game mechanic? When it's talking about "[my] copy of Soul Cemetary" does it mean the zine I'm holding in meatspace or my character clutching the Gamecube case inside my mind as I read? I don't think this ambiguity is a flaw or even a mistake. I get the sense from the expert typography, art and layout of Soul Cemetary that Snow (the developer) could have put more explicit instructions if she wanted to. No, what we're dealing with here is horror of a rare breed. This sense of not knowing what you're looking at, where the game of Soul Cemetary, the one you download of Itch and print out on your shitty inkjet you made as a panic buy to apply for HRT years ago and stapled together, or the fictional Capcom reject described by that book ends? That's horror right there. I got an intimidating feeling in my gut trying to fathom it on a first read through.

But, I did eventually fathom it. I got a strong sense of what this manual wanted me to do. I turned out all the lights in my house. I made a thread on a Discord server pretending to have discovered it in my childhood home. Having had previous experience roleplaying digitally with Alice is Missing I changed my server alias to my character name, put on a longplay of Silent Hill 2 as my one permitted light source and set to work writing in character reactions to the game that never was. The game's official soundtrack and it literally being around 1AM when my playthrough started did wonders. But, I started to feel that something was missing.

Every time I wanted to show off a screenshot of the game in character or get more specific with the timing of my messages, I was simply unable to. Because Soul Cemetary the video game doesn't exist. There's a palpable sense of loss and melancholy that comes with that. The manual constantly asks you to remember. To ask yourself how much of the impact the game left on your character carried through into the present. But I'm not Halligan. I'm not Julie, my RPG character. I'm me. I didn't live either of their lives. I didn't play Silent Hill until I was in Sixth Form. If I'm nostalgic for any game from when I was 12 it's probably not something that would have given my strict christian family a strong sense of unease. Not having lived their life and being unable to fully do it justice makes me feel sad in retrospect. I guess that's kind of the point. You're following these prompts and ticking boxes without being fully connected to what you're doing.

And then the game drops a bombshell. On page 14, your character has a bathroom break out of your control. The one playing the video game in the book. You the human are forced to sit reading her putting down the controller, turning on the light. I turned it on in real life because it was the closest thing to an instruction for me on the whole two page spread. The prose thereafter is hypnotic, electric. It constantly asks you to imagine a face in the dark. What it felt like seeing it in the basement as a child. Pleading me to walk through a house that I am simply unable to. I don't want to spoil it any further because I'd be cribbing from an artist that has shown far more competancy in writing than I likely ever will. But I will say that, both times I read it, it chilled me to my core. The second time was when I noticed the following page, entirely empty and black, looked like a face because of the cheap stapler I used to bind the book creasing its pages. Later that night, I'd get an emotional flashback of the severe nichtephobia I felt when I was younger. I had a strong fear of the supernatural and reliving it as an adult was entirely too much to bear. I was lucky it didn't give me nightmares. I'm still thinking about it, even as I write this. Especially since having it on the sofa next to me as I type, I don't see it the same as I did in the glow of the TV.

If any of this sounds interesting to you, please for the love of all that is holy buy Soul Cemetary immediately. If nothing else it's a fun experiment and I look forward to getting my hands on an official physical copy if they ever do another print run. The way it blends narrative and metanarrative. The fact that it's trans as hell and really digs deep into the psychological reflection aspect of its key inspiration impressed me to no end. I look forward to digging into the author's other work.
